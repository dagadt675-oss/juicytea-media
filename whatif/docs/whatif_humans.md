# What if all humans disappeared?

Calm documentary narration, US English. About 118 words. Each line is one beat. Claims we could not source are not in the script.

1. Every person on Earth disappears at once.
   Premise of Alan Weisman, *The World Without Us* (Thomas Dunne / St. Martin's, 2007): people vanish at once, and the book follows what the built world does next.

2. Almost at once, the power fails, and the city lights go dark.
   Weisman, interviewed in "An Earth Without People," *Scientific American*: one of the first things that would happen is that the power would go off, including plants that shut down when nobody is watching them.

3. Within about two days, the subways flood without their pumps.
   Weisman, *The World Without Us*, via his interviews with New York City Transit. Peter Briffa: within 36 hours the system could fill. The *New York Times* (Oct 1, 2007) summarizes the same passage: without the pumps that remove about 13 million gallons a day, the subway would fill in a couple of days. NPR's interview with Weisman uses "about two days."

4. In a matter of months, the pavement cracks, and weeds follow.
   *Scientific American*'s account of Weisman's timeline: boulevards start to crack and buckle in a matter of months. In the same interview Weisman describes freeze-thaw opening cracks and seeds blowing in quickly. The book itself is more specific later (ailanthus heaving sidewalks within about five years); the line stays at the earlier, hedged stage.

5. Within about twenty years, flooded streets can begin to cave in.
   Weisman, quoted in the *New York Times* (Oct 1, 2007): within 20 years the water-soaked steel columns under Lexington Avenue corrode and buckle, and the avenue becomes a river. He told *Marketplace* the same window, about 15 to 20 years.

6. Over the following decades, many wooden houses fall apart.
   *Scientific American* summarizing Weisman: over the following decades many houses and office buildings collapse. The book's house chapter, as summarized on Wikipedia, is the mechanism: roofs leak, wood rots, nails rust, walls sag.

7. As centuries pass, rust can bring the steel towers down.
   Weisman in *Scientific American*: waterlogged steel buildings eventually topple. He does not give a single year for every tower, so the line stays hedged. The History Channel series *Life After People* (2008–2010) stages major steel structures failing on the order of one to two centuries.

8. A few centuries on, the great suspension bridges collapse.
   Published summaries of Weisman's Manhattan timeline put the suspension bridges at about 300 years (Kevin Nelstead, *GeoChristian*, June 27, 2007, recounting that timeline). *Life After People* shows cables failing sooner, on the order of a century. "A few centuries" covers Weisman's figure without pretending the shows agree.

9. Within about five centuries, neighborhoods turn back into forest.
   Wikipedia's summary of *The World Without Us*: Weisman concludes that residential neighborhoods would become forests within 500 years. The author's own book description says asphalt jungles give way to real ones.

10. Plastic and bronze can outlast every fallen building.
    The book's own description (worldwithoutus.com): plastic and bronze sculpture may be among the most lasting human traces. *Scientific American* summarizing Weisman: some common plastics might remain intact for hundreds of thousands of years. Wikipedia notes bronze statues among the longest-lasting evidence.

11. Copper may remain only as a thin red seam in the rock.
    The book's description: copper pipes and wiring would be crushed into mere seams of reddish rock. The line does not claim this happens at a specific year. The on-screen counter at this beat reads 10 million years, which is the horizon those same summaries give for bronze sculpture still surviving, not a dated prediction that the seam forms on that day.

12. Then the lights return, and the city is whole again.
    This line is the loop, not a factual claim. The picture cuts back to the opening city.

Population on screen is 8,200,000,000. That is the UN World Population Prospects 2024 figure of about 8.2 billion in 2024, not a daily census. https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/world-population-prospects-2024
